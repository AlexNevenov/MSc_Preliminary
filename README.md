# Asymmetric Model of Galactic Fountains for the Origins of Milky Way's Intermediate- and High-Velocity Clouds
*Preliminary results of my MSc thesis (University of Groningen, with Prof. F. Fraternali and F. J. Urbina Jiménez).*

My thesis asks *'Why are intermediate- and high-velocity clouds (IVCs and HVCs) asymmetric between the northern and southern hemispheres of the Milky Way?'* To answer it, I run dynamical galactic-fountain simulations and produce Sun-centered (l, b, v) datacubes. I then compare, contrast, and fit these to observed all-sky HI datacubes. So far, I have done this extensively for the Leiden/Argentine/Bonn (LAB) survey ([Kalberla et al. 2005](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0504140)), so that we can make comparisons with earlier work such as [Marasco et al. (2012; hence *"Marasco+12"*)](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2012MNRAS.419.1107M).

The work comes in two parts. **Part A** (Notebooks 1–7) uses a mirror-symmetric model (the northern cube is mirrored into the south) as the baseline. **Part B** (Notebooks 8–14) is the first step towards the asymmetric treatment: the hot corona may move relative to the disk (as in the simulations of Oprea et al. 2026) and may differ between the hemispheres (as seen by eROSITA), so the clouds feel a different drag and a different relative speed in the north and the south.

This work is in progress and not peer reviewed yet. This repository contains notebooks for selected parts of the work (last updated on 8 November 2026), so that you can see what I have done so far, my workflow, and the conclusions I have drawn. The abstract (PDF) was written on 26 October 2026. It describes the symmetric baseline and lists the hemisphere treatment as the next step. Part B documents the first results of that step.

## Current state
- The preference for condensation-driven accretion is recovered robustly, as in Marasco+12 (symmetric baseline, Part A).
- Finer distinctions (e.g., neutral vs. ionized fountains, and the size of the differences) depend on how the model–data residual is defined. See Notebooks 1 and 3.
- Several fitted optima sit on the edge of the parameter box, so Notebook 5 includes three checks on how far they can be trusted.
- The hemisphere-asymmetric model is implemented and passes all 40 of its internal checks (Notebook 8). Fitted with the same grid search, the vertical motion of the hot corona (`v_motion`) comes out negative (southward-moving) for all nine residuals of Notebook 9. 
- No single residual is trustworthy on its own (they have no noise model, and react very differently to the choice of masks and normalization), so I look at trends across residuals. For some, one-sided masks and the north–south normalization of LAB can account for a large part of the gain, leaving a small part for the wind changing the shape of the emission. The trend I take away is a negative `v_motion` with some gain in most residuals, which I treat as tentative until the Bayesian step.
- Next: full Bayesian inference and model comparison. Notebooks 12–14 prepare for it (which likelihood, mask policy, and north–south normalization to use, and how wide the noise should be). The Bayesian analysis itself is work in progress and not yet in the repository.

### Part B: hemisphere-asymmetric model (Notebooks 8–14)
*Naming:* Internally, I number the work in steps. Step 3 is the symmetric baseline of Part A, Step 4 is the asymmetric model of Part B (the notebooks of Part B are labelled "Step 4, Part 1.x"), and Step 5 is the Bayesian analysis that is still to come. The grids are named by stage: `G1` is the symmetric grid of Notebook 5, `M1` has only the motion of the corona (`v_motion`, fitted), `C1` has only the south/north difference of the CGM (with `v_motion` fixed to −40 km/s), and `B1` (Notebook 9) has both. `M1` and `C1` were run with the same notebook as `B1` and other settings, so only the `B1` version is included, but their results enter Notebooks 11–13.

| [`8_asym_checks`](Notebooks/8_asym_checks.ipynb) | Does the asymmetric model do what it should? Then: how much of the residual is LAB's own north–south difference that no symmetric model can fit, what the south CGM ratios do one at a time (including a much hotter southern corona), whether a separate flux scale for each hemisphere explains the gain, where in (hemisphere, longitude, velocity) the asymmetric model beats the symmetric one, and a test in which each hemisphere gets its own wind. | The gain is small and depends on the residual: for the motion-only grid it ranges from about −4% to +10% relative to the symmetric fit (negative = better). | [`13_questions_for_bayesian`](Notebooks/13_questions_for_bayesian.ipynb) | The choices the Bayesian step has to make, each tested on the existing grids: the mask policy, the north–south normalization (one scale, own scales, a free ratio, or a prior on the ratio), the hyper-parameters of the candidate likelihood (`huber_sc`), how far to trust the Gaussian-process surrogate, the priors, and a closed-loop test on fake skies with a known wind, a known normalization difference, and a mask artefact. | One-sided masks and the north–south normalization can account for a large part of the gain of the asymmetric model, leaving a small part for the wind acting on the shape of the emission. The trend that survives is a negative `v_motion` with some gain in most residuals. How much the masks and the normalization matter depends on the residual: the robust `huber_sc` barely depends on the normalization treatment, whereas `abs_Li` depends strongly on it (its wind goes to +17 ± 20 km/s with a flux scale per hemisphere). | [`14_nonaxisym_for_bayesian`](Notebooks/14_nonaxisym_for_bayesian.ipynb) | How non-axisymmetric is LAB itself, and how wide is data − model? Following the idea of [Marasco+19](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201936338), the mirrored hemisphere is used as an empirical measure of what an axisymmetric model cannot be expected to reproduce. I measure the width of LAB north − south and its dependence on brightness, the width of data − model for the best symmetric and asymmetric models (split into common and antisymmetric parts), and what σ a Bayesian step should expect. | The scatter between LAB's hemispheres is dominated by sky structure, not instrument noise: the rms of (north − south)/√2 is about 93 mK (30 times the 3.1 mK noise), but its robust width is only about 5 mK, and it grows with brightness. Data − model is similar (rms 128 mK for the symmetric and 96 mK for the asymmetric model, robust widths 7.6 and 7.3 mK). A likelihood therefore needs a brightness-dependent, heavy-tailed (robust) width, and not the instrument noise. |

*Parameters:* `hv0` is the characteristic ejection (or 'kick') velocity (km/s), `alpha_conds` is the condensation rate (Gyr⁻¹, 0 = none), `alpha_hv` is the radial dependence of the ejection velocity (0 = constant), `f_ion` the ionization setting (0 = neutral fountain, 1 = ionized on the way up and neutral on the way down), and `vlag` is the lag (in km/s) of the corona relative to the local circular speed. 

*In Part B:* `v_motion` (km/s) is the vertical velocity of the hot corona in the rest frame of the disk. The northern run uses `+v_motion` (positive = towards the disk, a head wind for rising clouds) and the southern run `−v_motion`, so a negative value is a tail wind in the north and a head wind in the south, as in Oprea et al. 2026 (−40 km/s). The three `south_*_ratio` parameters set the southern value of the condensation rate, the drag, and `vlag` as the northern value times a ratio. They are fixed from the ~12% south–north temperature difference (first-pass values).
